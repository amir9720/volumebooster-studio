🎸 How to Boost Bass Safely

If you desire a much heavier low-end presence, focus your attention entirely on the 60Hz and 230Hz equalizer sliders. Moving the 60Hz slider up adds that deep, physical, subwoofer-style cinematic rumble. The 230Hz slider adds rich warmth and acoustic body to bass guitars and cellos. However, pushing these past +6dB can quickly muffle the rest of the song. A better studio trick? Cut the mid frequencies (910Hz) slightly instead of just boosting the bass.

🎙️ Improving Vocal Clarity on Podcasts

If you are listening to a recorded interview, university lecture, or amateur podcast where the recorded voices sound muffled, boxed in, or distant, you need to aggressively adjust the higher frequencies. Slightly boosting the 3.6kHz slider dramatically improves the presence, bite, and intelligibility of human speech, while the 14kHz (Treble) slider adds "air" and crispness to the consonants.

🎧 The "Scooped Mids" Rock Sound

If you are listening to heavy metal or hard rock, a very popular EQ curve is the "V-Shape" or "Scooped Mids". To achieve this aggressive tone, gently boost the 60Hz (Bass) and 14kHz (Treble) sliders by about +3dB to +5dB, while pulling the center 910Hz (Mid) slider down by -4dB. This pushes the vocals slightly back while making the kick drums and cymbals sound massive.

💡 Professional Audio Tip: Subtractive EQ over Additive EQ

Professional audio mixing engineers live by a very simple, yet crucial rule: It is almost always better to cut the bad frequencies you don't like, rather than boosting the good ones you do.

  • Avoiding Digital Distortion: Boosting sliders (a process known as Additive EQ) increases the overall volume gain of the track. If a song is already loud, this will push the audio signal past its digital ceiling (0dBFS), causing a harsh, crackling sound known as clipping.
  • The Subtractive EQ Method: If a song lacks bass, do not touch the bass sliders. Instead, try lowering the Treble (14kHz) and Mid (910Hz) sliders by -3dB first, and then turn up the main volume on your speakers. You will get a deep, bass-heavy mix without any risk of distortion.
