The Fascinating World of Backmasking and Reversed Sound

Have you ever listened to an eerie transition in a movie trailer where a cymbal seems to swell backwards out of nowhere, or wondered what your favorite vocal melody sounds like when flipped end to start? Reversing sound—often called backmasking—has a rich history stretching back to the tape-splicing experiments of early 1960s psychedelic rock.

When you play a natural sound backwards, you invert its acoustic envelope. Sounds in the physical world normally have a sharp, instantaneous onset (the attack) followed by a gradual decay. Flipping this envelope creates a long, swelling crescendo that terminates abruptly, turning ordinary door slams, guitar strums, or piano chords into surreal, dreamlike sound effects.

How In-Memory Array Reversal Works

Unlike old analog tape decks that required physical spool rewinding, digital audio is simply a long series of numerical floating-point samples (usually 44,100 or 48,000 numbers per second). This tool uses the Web Audio API to decode your file into raw Pulse-Code Modulation (PCM) audio buffers, traverses each channel array, and reverses the sample indices mathematically in temporary RAM. The reversed buffer is then synthesized into a fresh WAV file.
