Frequently Asked Questions

Wired headphones transmit electrical signals almost instantly. Bluetooth transmits audio as data packets over radio bands. Encoding the audio, transmitting the data, and decoding it inside the earbuds introduces a buffer delay (often between 50ms to 250ms).
Many video streaming apps (like Netflix and YouTube) use automatic delay compensation. They deliberately hold back the visual video stream so it aligns with the delayed Bluetooth audio. Interactive tasks like gaming cannot be pre-buffered, which exposes the true hardware latency.
Try enabling 'Low Latency' or 'Gaming Mode' in your earbud's companion app if available. Switching to codecs like aptX Adaptive or LDAC (if your hardware supports them) can also help reduce buffer delays.

Understanding Bluetooth Codecs and Acoustic Desynchronization

Audio-visual desynchronization can be irritating. You watch a character speak on screen, but the sound hits your ears a split second later. Or you tap an action button in a game, and the sound effect is delayed. In acoustic engineering, this disconnect is termed AV latency.

While wired headsets provide effectively zero latency, cutting the cord introduces digital processing. Before audio plays from your wireless earbuds, your device must compress the sound into a Bluetooth codec, transmit it as radio frequency packets, buffer it in the receiver chip, and convert it back to analog audio. Using an online Xiaomi earbuds latency test allows you to evaluate this delay.

Common Bluetooth Codecs and Latency

The specific codec negotiated between your smartphone and your audio receiver influences the total transmission latency:

  • SBC (Subband Codec): The universal baseline codec. Typical latency ranges from 150ms to 250ms. It is fine for music but can be sluggish for gaming.
  • AAC (Advanced Audio Coding): Standard on many devices. While it delivers good audio quality, latency can vary between 120ms and 200ms depending on the hardware.
  • aptX Low Latency & aptX Adaptive: Engineered specifically for interactive media, these protocols aim to drop buffer windows closer to 40ms to 80ms.
